# How the approaches hold up

Generated by `HEADLESS=1 python robustness.py`, which runs 6600 trials in about 2.2 hours on 7 workers. The tables below the marker are its
output; the text here is the interpretation. `python figures.py` draws the figures in `results/figures/` from the same rows, and every number
in them is in a table here.

These are the moving-start versions of A and B, the headline in `results/approach_b.md`: both start on the baseline's plan, and every solve
after it is charged. **That was chosen after seeing results, so the team should agree to it, and the paper should say so.** Each factor
moves on its own around the nominal cell, and toss noise also moves together with sensor noise. Trial n is the same toss and sensor noise in
every row, so approaches are compared in pairs, with McNemar's exact test.

**Read the p-values with care.** The primary comparison is B against A at the nominal cell: 25 only B and 0 only A (p = 6e-8), and 20 / 0
(p = 2e-6) on trials 100 to 199, which the cell wasn't chosen from. Everything else is dozens more tests with no correction for multiple
comparisons, so a p near 0.05 among them is a hint. The 0.12 m/s level of lateral toss noise was added after seeing 0.08.

## Findings

1. **B holds up, and A pays off only in the middle of the range.** B catches at least 47 of 100 in every noise cell, and the only one where it
   loses a toss that the baseline or A catches is σ_p = 20 mm, 6 to the baseline and 2 to A. A beats the baseline only when the sensor is good
   and the toss is noisy, as 2 and 3 show.
2. **Sensor noise has the shape B's spec predicted (§12), but it peaks early, and narrows for other reasons.** The gap is 0, 25, 59, 53 and 40
   tosses only B (with 2 only A at the last) at 0, 2, 5, 10 and 20 mm: none with a perfect sensor, then one that opens and narrows. The spec
   expected the peak at 10 mm and the narrowing from B running out of reach.
   - **A recovers from 24 to 37 because its correction stops happening.** At 10 and 20 mm the solver can't reach A's noisy target on 60 and
     79 tosses, which stay on the baseline's plan. They give 30 of A's 31 catches at 10 mm and all 37 at 20 mm. Where A did correct, it caught
     1 of 40 and 0 of 21. So at high sensor noise A is the baseline, less what its corrections throw away.
   - **B doesn't run out of reach.** It stays on the baseline's plan on 9 to 15 tosses at every level, as at 2 mm. At 20 mm it loses catches
     to its estimate instead, with a median predicted miss of 1.3 cm and 10 ms of timing error at 1 sigma.
3. **Toss noise doesn't widen the gap, and A's one correction hurts when the toss is quiet.** B's spec predicted a gap growing up to 0.08 m/s
   and then collapsing. It stays at 24 to 26 only B and none only A from 0 to 0.04 m/s, then narrows to 20 at 0.08 and 14 at 0.12 (p = 1e-4),
   rather than collapsing. A's loss to B comes from its estimate, a median predicted miss of 1.5 to 1.7 cm at every level up to 0.04 m/s,
   which toss noise doesn't change. What grows is the number of tosses neither can reach: contact falls to 82 and then 72 for all three, and
   at 0.12 m/s both stay on the baseline's plan on about half the tosses.
   - **At 0 and 0.01 m/s A misses 23 and 26 tosses that the baseline catches,** and catches 1 and 4 that it misses (p = 3e-6 and 6e-5). They
     are even at 0.02 m/s (10 / 18, p = 0.18), and A wins only from the nominal 0.04 m/s up (28 / 10, p = 0.005). A applies a correction with
     about 1.5 cm of error whether or not the toss needed one. B never does worse than the baseline here. Gating A's correction on its own
     uncertainty (B's spec, §15 Q3) might fix this, but it would change A, so it hasn't been tried.
4. **Vertical toss noise barely matters** to A or B, at 62 to 65 and 85 to 87 of 100. It costs the baseline, from 45 down to 34.
5. **The gripper's timing matters more than any noise.** The trigger still assumes 30 ms here.
   - At a real latency of 0 ms nothing is caught: the fingers close 30 ms early and the ball lands on them, with contact on 99.
   - At 20 ms, closing 10 ms early, B keeps 65 of 100, A 24 and the baseline 17.
   - At 50 ms, closing 20 ms late, B keeps 1, A 12 and the baseline 26. **This is the one cell where B does clearly worse than A,** 0 only B
     and 11 only A (p = 0.001). B's spec (§12) asks for that to be reported rather than engineered away. The likely mechanism is that B puts
     the pads where the ball is, so a ball the late fingers miss passes straight through. The baseline's and A's catches here are of tosses
     they had placed badly: the caught ones have a larger median predicted miss than the missed ones, 2.7 against 2.1 cm for the baseline and
     2.1 against 1.6 for A, and the ball ends 2.7 to 3.2 cm past the pinch point. That fits a ball slowed by hitting a finger first, which
     hasn't been checked in simulation.
   - 10 ms late wasn't run. **A real gripper's latency would have to be measured, not assumed.**
6. **A velocity sensor nearly closes the gap.** With position and velocity, A catches 81 and B 84, 3 / 0 (p = 0.25). A's median predicted
   miss halves, from 1.69 to 0.92 cm, and B's stays near 0.15 cm. So most of B's edge at the nominal cell is in estimating the velocity from
   positions, which it does better by watching for longer.
7. **With both noises together, the control holds and the pattern repeats.** With a perfect sensor A and B tie at every toss noise, 0 / 0.
   B stays between 72 and 100 across the grid. A falls with sensor noise at every toss noise, and drops below the baseline at 2 mm when the toss
   is quiet (72 against 80 at 0.02 m/s), but only at 5 mm when it is noisy (16 against 21 at 0.06 m/s).
8. **B's misses at the nominal cell are out of reach.** Its 13 misses are exactly the 13 tosses where every correction failed and the arm
   stayed on the baseline's path, and it caught all 87 tosses it moved its target on. They include 10 of the 11 tosses that need the most
   movement, 42.2 cm or more from where the brake leaves the pinch point. The baseline catches mostly the middle of that range: 35 of its 44
   catches are in the middle two quarters.
9. **A's misses don't depend on how far the ball moved.** B's spec (§11.3) expected A's catches to fall off where B had to move its target
   most. On the 87 tosses where B moved it, A caught 14, 14, 16 and 18 of about 22 in each quarter, from the smallest moves to the largest,
   with no significant trend (permutation p = 0.29). A misses because of its estimate, whatever the size of the move. B caught every one.
10. **The on-pad count is fragile, so report where the ball ends beside it.** A noiseless catch is always perched: every approach catches all
    100 and none on the pad, because the ball ends 1.47 cm along the fingers from the pinch point and the pad reaches 1 cm. B's catches end at
    a median of 0.95 cm, right at that edge, so small shifts swing the count: 1 of B's 85 catches with no vertical toss noise (a median of
    1.41 cm), 48 at 0.02 m/s and 62 at 0.05 m/s (0.78 cm). Where the ball ends is mostly set by when the fingers close: closing 10 ms earlier
    moves it a median of 0.97 cm further toward where the ball comes from, on the 65 tosses B caught both ways. Why vertical toss noise moves it
    hasn't been worked out. Retuning the trigger's 3 ms lead to center a noiseless catch would change every result, so it hasn't been done.
11. **The proposal's goal is met.** With no noise each approach catches and touches all 100, against the proposal's 50% caught and 90%
    contact. At the nominal cell contact is 98 for all three, and B catches 87 (79 to 92%). No catch anywhere in the sweep goes past 1.05x of
    rated speed; the worst is 1.04x.
